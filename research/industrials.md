# Industrials: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Total returns; sources in `events_group1.json`.*

## Preamble: 1999-2005

Industrials entered the century as the "old economy" and suffered with the tech bust anyway: XLI lost 10% in 2001 and 25% in 2002 as business investment collapsed, and 9/11 broke the airlines (UAL -42% and US Airways -52% on the first trading day; United, US Airways, Delta and Northwest all filed Chapter 11 by 2005). The recovery came from China. From the March 2003 low, Chinese infrastructure and mining demand made heavy machinery the leadership group of the bull market: Caterpillar returned 86% in 2003, Deere 99% in 2007, and XLI beat the S&P 500 in 2003-04.

## 2007-2010: crash and early-cycle rebound

Industrials fell further than the market in the GFC. XLI's drawdown was 62% (October 2007 to March 2009) against 55% for SPY, and the ETF lost 39% in 2008: United Rentals -50%, Deere -58%, Boeing -50%. The defensive wing held: Waste Management was positive in 2008 and Lockheed fell only 19%, because garbage is contracted and the defense budget was still growing with two wars.

The rebound was equally lopsided. Once credit reopened, capital rotated back into the names it had abandoned: XLI returned 22% in 2009 and 28% in 2010, United Rentals rose 132% in 2010, Deere 45% and 56%. A second China mining boom took Caterpillar up 68% in 2010; Caterpillar's own 2015 restructuring deck dates that cycle's peak to 2012, after which sales fell 31%.

## 2011-2014: sequestration, the Boeing supercycle, housing

The August 2011 Budget Control Act created the threat of automatic defense cuts; primes traded at valuations "not seen since the end of the Cold War" and federal services stocks lagged for two years. The 2013 lesson is worth remembering: the sequester took effect on March 1, 2013 and defense had its best year in decades (ITA +56%, Lockheed +68%) because the worst case was priced and the companies bought back stock. Commercial aerospace ran its own supercycle: Boeing lifted 737 output to 38 a month on a $415bn backlog and returned 85% in 2013. Building products joined on the housing recovery and QE3's record-low mortgage rates (PKB +41% in 2013).

## 2015-2016: the industrial recession

The oil crash, a 20% dollar rally and China's slowdown produced a manufacturing recession inside an expansion. Fastenal's CEO said in October 2015 that 44 of its top 100 customers were shrinking, railroads entered a bear market on coal and crude carloads, and Dow Transports lagged the S&P 500 by 1,800bp. Caterpillar, United Rentals and Old Dominion each lost 23-29% in 2015; the trough came in February 2016.

## 2016-2019: reflation, GE, tariffs, the MAX

Trump's election triggered the sharpest rotation into industrials since 2009: the group rose 4.4% in three sessions and 9% in a month on infrastructure and tax-cut hopes, Caterpillar returned 42% in 2016 and 75% in 2017, and XLI gained 24% in 2017. The February 2018 Bipartisan Budget Act lifted defense to $700bn and $716bn, 15% above the caps.

Two shocks ended the decade. GE halved its dividend in November 2017 as its gas-turbine business collapsed, fell 57% in two years and left the Dow in June 2018. The 2018 tariffs made Boeing and Caterpillar the trade-war proxies: XLI lost 13% in 2018 against 5% for the index, United Rentals fell 40% and Caterpillar's record profits could not stop a 12% slide. The 737 MAX grounding in March 2019 (Boeing -11% in two days, 20 months on the ground) removed the sector's growth engine just before COVID.

## 2020-2021: pandemic, vaccine, goods boom

COVID split the sector. JETS fell 59% between February 21 and March 23, 2020 (SPY -33%), United lost 49% in March, Berkshire sold its airlines and Boeing lost two-thirds of its value peak to trough. Defense sat out the entire 2020-21 bull market (ITA -14% then +9% against +18% and +29% for the S&P 500) as budgets flattened and ESG mandates excluded weapons makers, which set up the 2022 re-rating. The November 9, 2020 vaccine news began a reopening rotation that became 2021's goods boom: Old Dominion +85%, Robert Half +81%, United Rentals +43%, ADP +43%, XLI +21%. The IIJA, signed November 15, 2021, added a decade of funded demand; PAVE has returned more than 96% since.

## 2022-2025: Ukraine, rates, freight recession, AI power

Russia's invasion on February 24, 2022 ended defense's drought: Lockheed and Northrop each returned about 40% in a year the S&P 500 lost 18%. The rate shock (mortgages from 3% to 7%) de-rated building products (PKB -22%) while waste absorbed the defensive bid (WM -4.5%). Freight rolled over in mid-2022 and stayed depressed for three years; Old Dominion lost money in 2022, 2024 and 2025.

The dominant rotation began with Nvidia's May 2023 guidance: everything that powers and cools data centers re-rated. Vertiv returned 252% in 2023 and 137% in 2024, Eaton 56% and 40%, EMCOR 111% in 2024, and GE Vernova, spun out in April 2024, returned 99% in 2025. Comfort Systems' backlog doubled to $17.8bn; DeepSeek's January 27, 2025 scare (GE Vernova -18%, Vertiv -24% intraday) was fully recovered.

Policy drove 2025. Boeing's door-plug blowout, FAA cap and eight-week strike cost it 32% in 2024. Defense whipsawed on Hegseth's 8% cut memo (February) and Trump's $1 trillion budget (April), then re-rated globally on ReArm Europe (€800bn, March 4) and NATO's 5% target (June 25): ITA returned 49%. Liberation Day tariffs hit heavy equipment and transports hardest before the 90-day pause; XLI still finished 2025 up 19%. Professional services went the other way: Robert Half lost 59% and ADP 10% as investors priced AI agents replacing hiring and payroll seats, a selloff that climaxed on February 3, 2026 when Anthropic's Claude Cowork plug-ins triggered a $285bn one-day rout.

## 2026: war, rates, midterms

The Iran war that began February 28, 2026 roughly doubled jet fuel; airlines cut capacity and 2026 EPS estimates were slashed (United from $13.33 to $9.46) before fare increases produced a June rebound. Defense rallied on Operation Epic Fury, a $200bn supplemental and a $1.5 trillion FY2027 request (Lockheed +21% year to date), then fell 7% the day after the November 3 Democratic sweep. Staffing staged the year's biggest squeeze (ManpowerGroup +120% from its March low by August). Boeing fell 10% on October 27 after pushing its 737 ramp out a year, and the Fed's September 16 hike plus a 5.1% ten-year hit building products. XLI is up about 6% against 8% for the S&P 500: a stock-picker's market rather than a sector rotation.

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Defense.** The topline is a political variable: a $1.5 trillion request, a $901bn baseline, a House number near $1.15 trillion and a Democratic Congress. Stocks trade on reconciliation money for Golden Dome and munitions, European procurement under the 5% target, and Iran supplementals; the pure primes are more volatile than at any time since 2011.

**Commercial aerospace.** Boeing's production stability and free cash flow: the 737 ramp to 47/month slipped into 2027, 777X certification is mid-2027, the 2026 delivery target was missed. GE Aerospace, TransDigm and HEICO trade on flying hours and aftermarket pricing, which stay strong because the fleet is old and new jets are late.

**Transportation.** Airlines trade on jet fuel against premium-cabin demand; trucking and intermodal on whether the three-year freight recession ends; rails on volumes and tariff-driven import patterns. Cheap, but without a catalyst since 2021.

**Machinery.** A split: data-center power generation and mining are strong, construction is rate-sensitive, and ag is emerging from a two-year farm-income downturn (Deere +44% year to date). Steel and component tariffs remain the margin variable.

**Electrical equipment and electrification.** Hyperscaler capex guidance, grid spending and transformer lead times. Backlogs are at records (GE Vernova heading above $200bn), so the debate is multiple and political risk to data-center siting; capex scares produce 15-25% one-day drawdowns.

**Industrial distribution and rental.** Short-cycle production and non-residential construction (mega-projects, data centers); United Rentals is up 30% in 2026 on reshoring work, Fastenal and Grainger on pricing pass-through and the rate-sensitive pipeline.

**Building products and construction/engineering.** Two markets: EMCOR, Comfort Systems and Quanta trade on data-center and grid backlog; HVAC and residential names trade on 7%-plus mortgages and housing turnover. IIJA outlays still flow, with less than half the money awarded by early 2025.

**Professional and commercial services.** The AI-substitution debate is the whole story; the 2026 rebound says the market over-shot, but every model release reprices the group. Float income on client funds gives ADP and Paychex a positive rate sensitivity.

**Waste and environmental services.** A low-volatility compounder that trades as a defensive factor, outperforming in drawdowns (2008, 2022) and lagging in risk-on rallies (2023, mid-2025). Pricing above CPI, landfill scarcity and renewable natural gas are the fundamentals; the premium multiple is the risk.
