# Consumer Discretionary: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Companion data: `research/events_group2.json` (ids `cd-`). Series: XLY; XHB/ITB, CARZ, PEJ, XRT, FDN.*

## Preamble, 1999-2005: bubble, war, housing, Detroit

Four separate stories. Internet retail was the heart of the dot-com bust: the Nasdaq peaked at 5,048.62 on March 10 2000 and fell 78% by October 2002 while capital rotated into "old economy" consumer names. September 11 2001 shut markets for a week and crushed travel and hotels (New York occupancy below 40%, $15 billion of airline aid). The Fed's 1% rates created the 2003-2005 housing boom, in which builders roughly tripled before peaking in July 2005 and falling 24% by November. And Detroit's decline became a credit event when S&P cut GM and Ford to junk on May 5 2005.

## 2006-2009: the housing bust and the auto bankruptcies

Housing led the sector into the crisis: prices peaked in July 2006 and builder stocks fell more than 70% by 2008 (Toll -62%, Pulte -73% from the peak), with furniture and home improvement following. Specialty retail broke when Circuit City filed on November 10 2008 and liquidated in January. Autos were last: GM and Chrysler took $17.4 billion of federal aid in December 2008, Chrysler filed on April 30 2009 and GM on June 1 with $173 billion of debt. The policy response (the $8,000 homebuyer credit, Cash for Clunkers in July-August 2009) pulled demand forward and marked the lows; Ford, which avoided bankruptcy, became the recovery vehicle.

## 2009-2016: the long discretionary bull market

From the March 2009 low, consumer discretionary was the best-performing sector for most of the decade, and the inflows were about two things: cheap money (autos sold on sub-prime-style financing, builders recovered as rates stayed at zero, with the real housing recovery arriving in 2012) and the internet. Amazon's share of retail grew every year, and the sub-industry's premium multiple was increasingly a bet that Amazon could enter any category. Restaurants had their own growth cycle, and its end was single-name: Chipotle's E. coli and norovirus outbreaks of October-December 2015 cut revenue from $4.5 billion to $3.9 billion and net income from $476 million to $23 million the following year, a lesson in what food-safety risk does to a 40x multiple.

## 2017-2019: Amazon versus everyone, then tariffs

2017 was the "retail apocalypse": a record roughly 8,000 store-closure announcements and XRT down 8.6% through November 9 while the S&P 500 rose 15%. Amazon's June 16 2017 purchase of Whole Foods took $29 billion out of grocers and mass merchants in a day. Within specialty retail, money rotated to off-price, auto parts and home improvement, the categories Amazon could not easily take. The trade war then gave apparel a new driver: the August 1 2019 announcement of tariffs on the remaining $300 billion of Chinese goods (15% effective September 1 on $112 billion) accelerated sourcing shifts to Vietnam, the exposure that would matter in 2025.

## 2020-2021: COVID's two rotations

The pandemic produced the sharpest sub-industry dispersion in the sector's history. Norwegian fell 26.7% on March 9 2020 alone, the three cruise lines lost about $54 billion (70%) of value in under two months and then raised tens of billions of emergency debt and equity, which is why their shares never regained 2019 levels. Mall apparel failed in a wave (J.Crew, Neiman Marcus, JCPenney in May 2020) as April retail sales fell a record 16.4%. The winners were the home (Home Depot's Q2 sales +23.4%, Lowe's stock +65% in a year), e-commerce and Tesla (+743% in 2020, S&P 500 entry December 21). The second rotation began on November 9 2020 with Pfizer's vaccine: cruise lines and casinos rallied, Amazon fell 5%. The January 2021 GameStop squeeze ($17 to $483 intraday, 140% short interest) was the retail-flow extreme of that liquidity regime.

## 2022-2023: rates and the goods-to-services shift

2022 undid the pandemic trade. Amazon fell 51%, its worst year since 2000, dropping below $1 trillion; Tesla fell 68%. Mortgage rates hit 21-year highs and ITB lost 28.9% and XHB 26.3% while the S&P 500 fell 18%. Retailers that had over-ordered goods (Target, Walmart, Gap) took margin shocks in May 2022, and spending moved to services and travel. 2023 then inverted the housing trade: with existing homes locked up by low in-place mortgages, builders captured transactions through rate buy-downs, XHB was up 30% and ITB 38% by autumn and both hit records on the December 2023 Fed pivot. The October 2023 GLP-1 scare (Walmart's "buying less" comment) briefly dragged restaurants, snacks and beer.

## 2024-2025: brand fatigue, tariffs and the low-income consumer

Nike's worst day ever (June 28 2024, -20%, $28 billion) summarised apparel's shift from mega-brands to challengers (On, Hoka, Deckers) and off-price. The 2025 tariff regime then hit the sector harder than any other: 25% on imported vehicles and parts (March 26; GM -7%), the April 2 "Liberation Day" tariffs with 46% on Vietnam (Nike -10 to -13%; Lululemon, Deckers, Gap, American Eagle and Wayfair down up to 11%), the end of de minimis (China April 2, all countries August 29), and the OBBBA's termination of EV credits after September 30 2025, which produced a Q3 pull-forward and a Q4 cliff for Rivian, Lucid and the legacy EV programs. Dollar General's 32% collapse in August 2024 had already flagged a stretched low-income consumer, and by 2025 quick-service restaurants and discounters traded on that cohort's traffic.

## 2026: tariff relief, then a stall

The Supreme Court's February 20 2026 decision that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs voided the reciprocal duties: consumer discretionary rose about 1% that day, Amazon, Etsy, Tapestry and Williams-Sonoma gained, apparel popped because roughly 60% of its applied tariffs were IEEPA-based, and the Court of International Trade ordered refunds on April 20. The administration replaced them with a 10% Section 122 global tariff from February 24 and new Section 301 duties from July, and Section 232 tariffs on autos and parts were untouched. Despite the relief, the sector has stalled: XLY set an all-time high of 123.75 on January 12 2026 and was down 2.7% year-to-date at the end of October (roughly flat by early November after a 6% October bounce) while consumer staples rose 17%. With Amazon and Tesla dominating the fund, that stall reflects scepticism about Amazon's AI capex, the post-credit EV cliff, and a defensive rotation away from a weakening low-income consumer.

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Internet and direct retail.** Amazon trades on the AWS/AI capex-versus-retail-margin debate and logistics cost deflation; the marketplaces (Etsy, eBay) on the tariff regime, where the February 2026 IEEPA ruling and April refunds were a windfall, Section 122/301 duties are the new base case, and the end of de minimis helps domestic sellers against Temu and Shein. The Chinese platforms trade on Beijing's stimulus.

**Autos and auto parts.** Affordability (auto-loan rates and the Fed path), the Section 232 25% vehicle and parts tariffs that survived the IEEPA ruling, the EV demand cliff after the September 30 2025 credit expiry, and Tesla's robotaxi/AI narrative, which has decoupled it from unit economics. Mexico-exposed GM carries the most tariff beta.

**Homebuilders and home improvement.** The 30-year mortgage rate and the Fed's cutting cycle are the master variable; builders earn outsized share while existing inventory is locked in but give back margin through incentives. Lumber and tariff input costs, first-time buyer policy and the 2022-2023 lesson that scarce supply can trump rates are secondary; home improvement waits for turnover.

**Travel, leisure and restaurants.** Cruise lines and hotels trade on record bookings against the 2020 debt and dilution, international travel and the dollar; casinos on Macau and Las Vegas. Restaurants trade on low-income traffic (the value war since 2024), wage inflation and, episodically, GLP-1 headlines, which returned with the November 2025 price cuts.

**Apparel, footwear and luxury.** Tariff policy dominates: the 46% Vietnam rate is gone but 10% Section 122 and Section 301 duties remain and refunds are flowing. Beyond trade, the group trades on brand heat (challengers versus Nike), China luxury demand (weak since 2023) and wholesale-versus-DTC mix.

**Specialty and off-price retail.** Off-price (TJX, Ross, Burlington) trades on trade-down, amplified by 2025-2026 consumer stress; auto parts on the ageing fleet; the rest on tariffs, mall traffic and card delinquencies. Import-heavy names (Five Below, Best Buy, Wayfair) were April 2025's biggest losers and February 2026's biggest winners.
