# Health Care: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Companion data: `research/events_group2.json` (ids `hc-`). Series: XLV; IHE, IBB, XBI, IHI, IHF; baskets for tools, managed care and health-care technology.*

## Preamble, 1999-2005: three regimes in six years

Health care entered the century as a defensive sector with a speculative appendage. Genomics stocks were bid up as the biological internet, and broke first: on March 14 2000 a Clinton-Blair statement on open genome data was misread as the end of gene patents and the Nasdaq Biotech Index fell 12.5% in a day. The rest of the sector held value through the 2000-2002 bear market. Two shocks then set the pattern for everything after: the Medicare Modernization Act (December 2003) created Part D and modern Medicare Advantage, and managed care re-rated for two years on its new subsidised market (UnitedHealth took 27% of Part D enrollment at launch); and Merck's Vioxx withdrawal (September 30 2004, stock -27% in a day) reminded investors that pharma cash flows carry binary safety risk. By 2006 pharma was a value trade, managed care a growth trade and biotech a venture trade.

## 2006-2012: the ACA years, hospitals versus insurers

The ACA fight created the rule that **hospitals and insurers trade against each other on coverage policy**. Obama's 2008 win took Cigna down 40% and WellPoint 15% within a month. When the House passed the law on March 21 2010, Tenet rose 9% and HMA 11% while UnitedHealth fell 3%: more insured patients for providers, margin caps and fees for commercial insurers. The June 28 2012 Supreme Court decision repeated the move (HCA +10.75%, big insurers -3 to -4%) and locked in a 2.3% device excise tax that lasted until its 2019 repeal. Hospitals and Medicaid insurers are long coverage, commercial insurers are short regulatory margin pressure, and the legs swap leadership on every election and ruling.

## 2012-2016: the biotech supercycle and its unwind

Hepatitis C cures, immuno-oncology, the 2012 JOBS Act and zero rates produced the sector's greatest bull market: IBB rose about 214% from January 2012 to mid-2015 and capital flowed out of pharma and devices into anything with a Phase 2 asset. The top was political. Hillary Clinton's September 21 2015 "price gouging" tweet cut IBB 4.6% in a day and 13% for the week, and drug-pricing politics, Valeant's collapse and the August 2015 China scare took XBI down 49% to February 2016, worse than 2008. SMID biotech is a liquidity and risk-appetite instrument first and a science instrument second.

## 2016-2019: election beta

Trump's 2016 win was read as ACA repeal: Tenet fell 25% and HCA 11% on November 9 2016 while pharma rallied. Repeal failed (the AHCA was pulled on March 24 2017; the Senate "skinny repeal" died on July 28) and heavily shorted hospitals squeezed back. The January 30 2018 Amazon-Berkshire-JPMorgan venture took $69 billion out of PBMs, pharmacies and insurers in a day. In April 2019, with Medicare for All gaining sponsors, UnitedHealth's CEO attacked it on his own earnings call and S&P 500 health care lost $150 billion in four days; "political beta" ruled managed care until the 2020 primaries.

## 2020-2021: pandemic winners

COVID split the sector. CMS told hospitals to postpone elective surgery on March 18 2020, so device makers and hospitals lost their revenue base while diagnostics and bioprocessing, telehealth (Teladoc $83 to $294) and mRNA vaccines (Moderna: $18.5 billion of 2021 revenue) absorbed the inflows. Pfizer's November 9 2020 efficacy news began the reopening rotation, and zero rates plus a record IPO/SPAC calendar pushed XBI to an all-time high on February 8 2021. Tools spent their windfalls on CROs (Thermo-PPD, $17.4 billion, April 2021).

## 2021-2023: the rate shock and the GLP-1 split

Rising real yields repriced long-duration health care first and hardest. XBI fell 63.9% from February 2021 to May 2022, the deepest drawdown in its history; Teladoc wrote down $13.4 billion of Livongo goodwill and fell 88% from its peak; tools took the second-order hit as biotech customers destocked (Danaher cut guidance in April 2023, Thermo in October). The Inflation Reduction Act (August 16 2022) added Medicare price negotiation. Meanwhile capital concentrated into GLP-1s: Novo became Europe's most valuable company in 2023 and Lilly's Zepbound was approved on November 8 2023. The same drugs created a "scare trade" on October 4 2023 when Walmart said GLP-1 users were buying less, hitting Dexcom (-30% year-to-date) and Insulet (-54%) and CPAP suppliers. And on June 14 2023 UnitedHealth flagged surging senior outpatient surgery, the start of the Medicare Advantage cost problem: insurers fell, Stryker and HCA rose.

## 2024-2025: managed care breaks, policy risk everywhere

2024 brought the Change Healthcare ransomware attack (February 21; $872 million of Q1 costs), a flat Medicare Advantage rate notice (April 2; insurers -6 to -12%), the first negotiated Medicare prices (August 15; 22% aggregate cut) and RFK Jr.'s HHS nomination (November 14; Moderna -5.6%). 2025 was worse. UnitedHealth cut guidance and fell 23% on April 17, lost its CEO on May 13, fell 13% more on May 15 on the DOJ criminal probe report, and ended the year down 35%; Centene pulled guidance on July 1 and fell 40%. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 4) cut Medicaid by about $1 trillion over ten years and the enhanced ACA subsidies drifted toward expiry through a 39-day shutdown. Pharma faced the MFN order (May 12), the 100% tariff threat (September 25) and company-by-company deals, culminating in the November 6 GLP-1 agreement ($245 a month for government programs, Medicare obesity coverage) that also killed compounded-GLP-1 telehealth (Hims had lost 35% in a day when Novo cut it off on June 23). Novo cut guidance on July 29; Lilly hit $1 trillion on November 21. NIH freezes and more than 5,800 grant terminations weighed on tools.

## 2026: biotech leads, devices lag, insurers whipsaw

Three things define 2026. Medicare Advantage: the January 26 advance notice proposed a 0.09% rate change and UnitedHealth and Humana lost a fifth of their value in a day; the April 6 final notice delivered 2.48% and they recovered 10-11% in a session. Tariffs: the April 2 Section 232 proclamation imposed 100% duties on patented drugs with 15% carve-outs for the EU, Japan, Korea and Switzerland and exemptions for companies that signed deals; because the Supreme Court's February 20 ruling voided IEEPA tariffs but not Section 232, this regime binds. Rotation: XBI is up about 25% year-to-date and IBB 27% as of November 3, while IHI is the only major health-care ETF in the red.

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Pharmaceuticals (IHE).** Trades on policy deal status more than pipelines: companies with MFN/TrumpRx and onshoring agreements (Lilly, Pfizer, J&J) are exempt from the 100% Section 232 tariff that bites from July-September 2026; European and Japanese exporters are not. GLP-1 is the only growth engine and the 2026-2030 patent cliff keeps M&A appetite high.

**Biotech, large cap (IBB).** Up 27% in 2026 on pipeline readouts, pharma M&A, the IRA negotiation calendar and FDA predictability. Vaccine and mRNA names stay de-rated by HHS policy; Moderna's revenue fell from $18.5 billion (2021) to $1.9 billion (2025).

**Biotech, SMID cap (XBI).** The sector's risk-appetite gauge. The 2026 rally (+25% YTD) rests on lower rates, reopened IPO windows and takeouts, but XBI has not regained its February 2021 high and cooled 1.5% in the past month. It lives and dies on real yields and FTC/FDA posture.

**Life sciences tools and services.** Recovering from the 2023 destocking; trades on biotech funding (improving with XBI), NIH budgets (about 2,600 grants worth $1.4 billion still suspended in January 2026), China demand and tariffs on instruments.

**Medical devices and equipment (IHI).** The 2026 laggard: sold on rotation into biotech, tariff exposure on imported components and recurring GLP-1 volume fears whenever GLP-1 prices fall. Procedure volumes remain solid, so the de-rating is positioning, not earnings.

**Managed care.** Trades on the CMS calendar: the January advance and April final Medicare Advantage notices (2026: -20% then +10%), October Star ratings, Medicaid rates after OBBBA, exchange morbidity after the subsidy cliff, and the DOJ's UnitedHealth investigations. Cost trend is the fundamental; policy is the volatility.

**Providers, facilities and distribution (IHF).** Hospitals trade on coverage: the $1 trillion Medicaid cut and subsidy expiry are a 2027-2034 volume and bad-debt headwind, partly offset by a $50 billion rural fund, while near-term volumes are favourable. Distributors ride GLP-1 volumes; PBM reform and 340B are the overhangs.

**Health care technology.** The pandemic cohort never recovered; the group trades on real yields, GLP-1 telehealth economics now that the November 2025 price deal has ended compounding, provider IT budgets and clinical AI adoption.
