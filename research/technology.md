# Technology: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Research note for the Capital Rotation Tracker (sector id `technology`; sub-industry ids from `pipeline/taxonomy.json`). Companion data: `research/events_group3.json`, `research/regimes.json`. Prepared 7 November 2026.*

## The short version

Technology is the sector the rest of the market rotates around. It fell 82% peak-to-trough in the dot-com bust, overtook financials as the largest S&P 500 sector in May 2008 and has not given that up. Three forces explain most of its moves: real interest rates (software, internet and any long-duration cash flow), its customers' capex cycle (semiconductors, memory, hardware, now the hyperscalers' AI build-out) and policy (export controls, tariffs, antitrust and the GICS reclassifications of 2018 and 2023 that moved companies between sectors).

## 1999-2007: bubble, bust and rebuild

The Nasdaq rose 85.6% in 1999 and peaked at 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000 with the Fed still tightening. Between 24 March 2000 and 9 October 2002 the S&P 500 technology sector lost 82.4% and telecom 74.1% against 49.2% for the index; Cisco, briefly worth more than $500 billion, lost about 80%. Emergency cuts from 3 January 2001 could not revive capex. The Nasdaq returned 50% in 2003 on 1% fed funds, then lagged for three years (2005: +1.4% versus +4.9%) as the June 2004-June 2006 hiking cycle sent capital to energy, materials and banks. The iPhone launch on 29 June 2007 was the first tech-specific catalyst in years: Apple went from roughly $77 to $200 in a year when the S&P 500 returned 5.5%.

## 2008-2012: resilience and the mobile-cloud build-out

The financial crisis was a rotation as much as a crash. Financials fell 82.6% peak-to-trough; technology fell 53%, less than the index, because its balance sheets held cash rather than mortgages. Qimonda's January 2009 insolvency marked the bottom of the DRAM cycle. With the Fed at zero and buying bonds (QE2 November 2010, QE3 September 2012), the smartphone and cloud build-out led: the Nasdaq returned 43.9% in 2009, smartphone shipments hit 725 million units in 2012 and a billion in 2013, and Apple set a record $624 billion market cap on 20 August 2012. Facebook's $38 IPO in May 2012, which halved by September, showed the market would still punish unproven monetisation.

## 2013-2017: taper, consolidation, election, melt-up

The May 2013 taper tantrum hit the sector's yield-sensitive corner (telecom +11.5% in 2013 versus 32.4% for the index) but not its growth corner. Dell's $24.4 billion LBO took legacy PC hardware private. Avago-Broadcom ($37 billion, May 2015) headlined a half-year of $72.6 billion in announced semiconductor deals, six times the prior annual average, the consolidation behind the industry's later pricing power. The 2016 election was a textbook rotation: banks had their best month on record (KBE +18.7% in November 2016) while Apple and the platforms fell on trade fears. Then 2017's synchronized growth and tax cut produced the melt-up: technology +38.8%, FAANG +46.5%, telecom the worst sector at -1.3%.

## 2018-2019: reclassification, trade war, Fed pivot

Three things happened in 2018. GICS moved Alphabet and Facebook out of IT and Netflix and Comcast out of Consumer Discretionary into Communication Services after the close on 28 September, reclassifying roughly 10% of index market cap, which is why this taxonomy keeps an `internet_platforms` bucket that spans GICS sectors. The trade war arrived: steel tariffs in March, Section 301 tariffs from July and, in May 2019, the Huawei entity listing that cost Micron a customer worth 13% of sales and dropped the semiconductor index about 14% in a month. And the Fed's 19 December hike capped a quarter in which the Nasdaq fell 17.5% and Apple more than 31%. The pivot (first cut 31 July 2019) and the Phase One deal reversed all of it: technology returned 50.3% in 2019, almost entirely multiple expansion.

## 2020-2021: pandemic winners and the speculative peak

COVID rewarded anything digital: technology +43.9% in 2020, Zoom up roughly 580% by November, PC demand +13%, Apple the first $2 trillion company on 19 August 2020. The 2021 chip shortage (15-week lead times, an estimated $210 billion of lost automaker revenue) gave semiconductors pricing power and a third straight year of outperformance at 6.3x forward sales. The speculative peak was in fintech and crypto: Coinbase listed on 14 April 2021 at a $47 billion reference valuation and closed day one at $328.28; bitcoin peaked three weeks before the Nasdaq.

## 2022: the rate shock

Liftoff on 16 March 2022 and eleven hikes repriced every long-duration asset. Technology returned -28.2%, communication services -39.9% and the Nasdaq -33.1% against -18.1% for the S&P 500 and +65.7% for energy. Software was roughly cut in half, Meta posted a record $230 billion one-day loss in February, Netflix fell 35% in a day in April, the F-Prime fintech index fell 72%, and Adobe's $20 billion Figma bid cost it 17% in a day. Policy added a second layer: the CHIPS Act (9 August 2022, $52.7 billion plus a 25% fab credit) pulled capital toward US fabs, and the 7 October 2022 export controls created a standing China discount on semicap names.

## 2023-2025: the AI trade

ChatGPT (30 November 2022) and Microsoft's $10 billion OpenAI investment (23 January 2023) set the narrative; Nvidia's 24 May 2023 guidance ($11 billion versus $7.15 billion expected, stock +26%, $1 trillion within a week) made its earnings the sector bellwether. Technology returned 57.8% in 2023 and 36.6% in 2024. Hyperscaler capex went from $37.8 billion a quarter in early 2023 to $140.6 billion in Q4 2025, and that spending drove everything downstream: Super Micro into the S&P 500 (then into a governance scandal), Vertiv +252% in 2023, DRAM prices +172% in 2025, Oracle's backlog +359% on a reported $300 billion OpenAI contract. The two shocks of 2025, DeepSeek (27 January, Nvidia -17%, a record $589 billion) and Liberation Day (2 April, S&P 500 -12% in four sessions before the 9 April pause and 11 April chip exemption), were both bought; the S&P 500 made a new high on 27 June 2025 and Nvidia reached $5 trillion on 29 October 2025. Policy turned from stick to carrot: payments moved to Financials (March 2023), the GENIUS Act legalised stablecoins (July 2025), the government took 9.9% of Intel (August 2025) and the January 2026 chip tariff came with exemptions for anyone building in America. The losers were labor- and seat-based models: software peaked in September 2025 and IT services were downgraded on AI-substitution risk and hit by the $100,000 H-1B fee.

## 2026: oil shock, chip melt-up, SaaS-pocalypse, credit question

Anthropic's Claude Cowork plug-ins and Opus 4.6 (2-6 February) erased roughly $300 billion of software value in a day and IGV reportedly fell 24% in Q1; Claude Managed Agents (8 April) took Akamai down 17%. The Supreme Court voided the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February, a relief for import-heavy hardware and Apple. The Iran war from 28 February sent Brent to $119.50 and made energy the year's leader, yet semiconductors had their best quarter ever in Q2 (SMH +71%, SOX +106% year-to-date, Micron a $1 trillion company on 26 May) on the thesis that AI needs memory and CPUs, not just GPUs. The reversal began on 1 July (Micron -11%) and broadened: Alphabet turned free-cash-flow negative (-7% on 23 July, -13% on 29 October), Accenture had its worst day (-18%, 18 June) and best (+22%, 1 October), CoreWeave's CDS passed 1,000 bp, states imposed data-center moratoria, the Fed hiked on 16 September, Intel's PC warning dropped the SOX 7% on 30 October, and the Democratic midterm sweep on 3 November raised moratorium risk over 45% of planned capacity. XLK fell 19.2% in the month to 5 November and is up 13.2% for the year, behind energy (+40.6%); the Nasdaq's 6 October record of 27,599.89 is the cycle high for now.

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Semiconductors.** Nvidia's data-center revenue and hyperscaler capex guidance, now filtered through credit: can OpenAI, CoreWeave and the neoclouds pay for what they contracted. Then export policy (the H200 case-by-case regime, the Hua Hong tool halt, Lutnick's 100% tariff threat), the consumer side (Intel's PC warning) and moratorium risk under a Democratic Congress.

**Semiconductor equipment.** Leads the chip cycle by two quarters on WFE outlooks, memory capex and China. Lam draws about 35% of revenue from China, so each BIS action is a repricing event; CHIPS-funded and tariff-offset fabs and Taiwan's $250 billion pledge are the offset.

**Memory and storage.** A pure price cycle: DRAM up 172% in 2025, capacity sold out through 2026 because HBM absorbed wafers. The debate is duration (Micron says through 2027, SK Hynix to 2030) versus demand destruction (PC shipments forecast -10-11% in 2026, Google's TurboQuant compression).

**Application software.** Whether AI agents shrink seats or expand consumption. Each Anthropic release has been a selloff; each quarter of stable net retention a relief rally (IGV +41% from 13 April to 12 October 2026, still down over one year). Consumption pricing, take-private interest and real yields are the swing factors.

**Infrastructure software and cybersecurity.** Azure and OCI growth, Oracle's single-customer backlog and BBB- rating, and the consolidation bid (Google-Wiz $32 billion, Palo Alto-CyberArk $25 billion). Security budgets are the least discretionary line in IT, but the April 2026 selloff showed edge and infrastructure names are not immune to the agent narrative.

**IT services and consulting.** The most AI-exposed labor model: moat downgrades, the H-1B fee, Accenture's record down and up days, EPAM's ejection from the S&P 500. Trades at 9-12x earnings on bookings and whether AI integration work offsets shrinking billable hours.

**Internet platforms and media tech.** Capex intensity versus free cash flow is the valuation axis: Alphabet lost $490 billion on 29 October 2026 despite 82% cloud growth. Antitrust appeals, LLM search disruption and the ad cycle are secondary; the sector that led 2024-25 is the worst of 2026.

**Hardware, networking and components.** AI server and optical demand against memory-cost inflation (Dell's downgrade, HP's memory at 35% of bill of materials) and governance risk (Super Micro). Tariff relief helps; 2026 unit forecasts hurt.

**Apple.** iPhone cycle, China, services and tariffs, with memory cost the newest input: the 25 June 2026 price increases cost 6%, yet Apple became a $5 trillion company on 28 July. Chip and smartphone exemptions and buybacks underpin the quality premium.

**Fintech and payments.** Housed in Financials by GICS but tracked here: stablecoin adoption under the GENIUS Act (Circle, Coinbase), the reopened IPO window (Circle, Klarna, Figma), consumer credit, interchange regulation and the Capital One-Discover network precedent; PayPal's 2026 takeover interest shows the legacy names trade as value.

**Telecom and cable.** A yield and consolidation trade: three-carrier wireless, spectrum consolidation (EchoStar's sales to AT&T and SpaceX), fiber M&A (Verizon-Frontier, AT&T-Lumen, Charter-Cox) and the bankruptcy of satellite TV. Rising long yields and the Fed hike cap the dividend argument; the sector trades on broadband share versus fixed wireless and on dividend coverage.
