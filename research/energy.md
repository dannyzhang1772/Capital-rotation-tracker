# Energy: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Total returns; sources in `events_group1.json`.*

## Preamble: 1999-2005

Energy was a 6% weight and a value backwater at the Nasdaq peak, and the first rotation of the century was into it: XLE returned 24% in 2000 while the S&P 500 lost 10%. The 2001-02 recession took oil back under $20, but from the March 2003 low the China supercycle made energy the market's leadership sector for five years. XLE beat the index every year from 2003 to 2007 (40% in 2005, 37% in 2007), the sector rose roughly 300% trough to peak, and its weight climbed toward 16% by mid-2008. Refining had its own golden age inside the boom: no new US refinery since 1976 plus Katrina and Rita in 2005 gave Valero 98% in 2004 and 128% in 2005.

## 2008-2010: $147 to $32 and the shale rebound

WTI peaked at $147 on July 11, 2008 and fell to about $32 by December. Leverage showed in the returns: XLE -39% in 2008, E&Ps (XOP) -43%, services (OIH) -60%, Valero -69%, while net-cash Exxon fell 13%. The 2008 solar bubble burst in parallel (TAN lost three-quarters of its value in five months). The rebound was led by beta (OIH +64% in 2009, XOP +40% and +29% in 2009-10). Shale also created the midstream yield trade: zero rates and new pipelines out of the Bakken and Marcellus pulled retail money into MLPs and made Cheniere, the first US LNG exporter, a three-time triple-digit stock.

## 2011-2013: solar's second bust and the refiners' spread

Chinese capacity collapsed module prices just as Europe cut subsidies, and Solyndra's bankruptcy on August 31, 2011 made solar politically toxic: TAN -63% in 2011, First Solar -74%. The sector rebounded 128% in 2013 on stable polysilicon and the yieldco model. For fossil energy the story was the Cushing glut: shale crude trapped mid-continent put WTI at a $15-22 discount to Brent in 2012, so refiners bought cheap and sold at world prices (Marathon Petroleum +89%, Valero +66% in 2012, Valero +65% in 2013).

## 2014-2016: OPEC's price war and the MLP collapse

With US shale adding a million barrels a day a year, OPEC refused to cut on November 27, 2014 and crude fell 10% that day; XOP was already 40% off its June high, and WTI bottomed near $26 in February 2016. XOP lost 29% and 36% in 2014-15 and XLE 21.5% in a flat 2015, with dozens of bankruptcies. The MLP model broke in the same crash: the Alerian index fell 47% and Kinder Morgan cut its dividend 75% on December 8, 2015. Refiners were again the only winners (Valero +47% in 2015) and SunEdison's April 2016 bankruptcy put solar back in a bust (TAN -43%). The turn came with OPEC's first cut since 2008 on November 30, 2016: XLE rose 5% in a day and finished 2016 up 28%, XOP 38%, KMI 43%.

## 2017-2019: over-production and the shrinking weight

Three years of volume growth over returns made energy the worst sector of the late-cycle bull market. XOP lost money in 2017, 2018 and 2019, OIH lost 45% in 2018 alone, and the Q4 2018 crash (Brent -37% from its October peak, Exxon -14% in December) came as the sector's weight fell toward 4%. Generalists exited, which is why the 2020-21 reversal was so violent.

## 2020-2021: negative oil, clean-energy mania, discipline rebound

The OPEC+ breakdown on March 6, 2020 and lockdowns took the sector down about 50% in Q1; on April 20 the May WTI contract settled at -$37.63, and on August 24 Exxon left the Dow with energy at 2.4% of the S&P 500. XLE lost 33% for the year. At the same moment zero rates, ESG inflows and the Biden platform produced the largest clean-energy bubble on record (ICLN +142%, TAN +234%, Enphase +572% in 2020), peaking in February 2021. The two halves then swapped: rising yields took ICLN down 24% in 2021 while reopening demand and the new shale model (flat volumes, buybacks, variable dividends) gave fossil energy its best year since 2005 (XLE +53%, XOP +67%, AMLP +39%, Exxon +58%).

## 2022-2025: Ukraine, the IRA, consolidation, the AI power trade

Russia's invasion made energy the only positive sector of 2022 (XLE +64% against -20%, the widest outperformance on record): Exxon +87%, SLB +81% as international and offshore work resumed, refiners at record margins (Marathon earned $28.17 a share) and LNG exporters re-rated by Europe's gas scramble. The Inflation Reduction Act (August 16, 2022) gave clean energy a brief rally and a decade of credits, but 5% yields and inventory gluts produced a third solar bust: TAN -27% in 2023 and -38% in 2024, Enphase -50% both years, and a further plunge when Trump won in November 2024.

With oil flat around $75, 2023-24 belonged to consolidation and gas. Exxon-Pioneer (~$60bn) and Chevron-Hess ($53bn) in October 2023 started a wave that shrank the public E&P universe; XLE was flat in 2023 and up 6% in 2024. Pipelines became an AI derivative (Kinder Morgan +64% in 2024) on data-center and LNG demand, and the nuclear/IPP trade was born: the July 2024 PJM auction clearing at $270/MW-day (from $29) and Constellation's Microsoft deal to restart Three Mile Island sent Vistra up 262% and Constellation 93% in 2024. DeepSeek knocked 20-30% off the IPPs on January 27, 2025 and Trump's May 23 nuclear orders sent Oklo up 130% in a month.

2025 was a down year for the commodity. OPEC+ tripled its planned hike the day after the Liberation Day tariffs, WTI hit $61.99 and oil spent the year in the $60s (XLE +8%, XOP -2% against +18% for the index); oilfield services hit multiyear lows while refiners decoupled on capacity closures (Valero +37%). Clean energy whipsawed on the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Sunrun -39% and -40% on the House and Senate drafts, +8% when leasing survived); First Solar ended up 48% on tariffs and AI power demand, Enphase down 53%.

## 2026: the Hormuz war

Israel struck Iran on February 28, 2026 and the US launched Operation Epic Fury. Drones hit Ras Tanura and Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG plant, insurers cancelled Hormuz war-risk cover and transits collapsed from 85 a day to near zero. Brent went from the $60s to $84 on March 3, $109 on March 9 and $115 in late October before easing toward $98-100 in November; the Fed hiked in September on the resulting inflation. Energy is 2026's best sector: XLE +41% year to date against 8% for the S&P 500, Cheniere +45% on the LNG premium, and refiners the biggest winners as diesel hit a record $5.90 a gallon (Valero +119%). The IPPs went the other way: after three record PJM auctions turned power prices into an affordability issue, Constellation and Vistra are down about 30%. The Democratic midterm sweep lifted solar 8-9% on November 4 from a low base (First Solar -23%, Sunrun -49% year to date).

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Integrated oil and gas.** Brent in the $95-115 range set by the Hormuz closure, and whether a settlement reopens the strait and takes $30-40 out of the price. Exxon and Chevron trade on free cash flow at $100 oil, buyback capacity and the Permian/Guyana growth they bought in 2023; they are the sector's defensive core.

**Exploration and production.** Pure commodity beta with capital discipline: XOP moves 1.5-2x oil. Gas-weighted names (EQT, Expand, Antero) trade on LNG feedgas and data-center load; oil names on OPEC+ policy once the war premium fades. Consolidation has left fewer, larger, lower-beta names.

**Refining and marketing.** Crack spreads and product shortages. With Gulf refineries damaged and diesel at records, US refiners capture the global product deficit; the risk is a ceasefire that normalizes margins as fast as 2024 did (CRAK -15%). Capacity closures and RIN costs are the structural variables.

**Midstream and pipelines.** Fee-based yield that trades on gas volume growth from LNG and data centers, permitting reform and the 10-year yield. It lagged the 2026 upstream rally because it has little oil exposure, but LNG exporters are direct beneficiaries of Qatar's outage.

**Oilfield equipment and services.** International and offshore capex is strong; North American pressure pumping still shrinks with the rig count and E&P discipline. OIH is up 22% in 2026 after two down years; the group trades on whether $100 oil finally lifts US drilling budgets.

**New energy, nuclear and independent power.** Three trades. Solar trades on policy (OBBBA phase-outs, December panel tariffs, a Democratic Congress against a presidential veto) and rates. Nuclear and uranium trade on SMR approvals, executive orders and hyperscaler PPAs. The IPPs trade on PJM capacity prices and the political backlash against them, which is why Constellation and Vistra fell 30% in a year of record power demand.
