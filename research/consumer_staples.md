# Consumer Staples: where the capital went, 1999-2026

*Companion data: `research/events_group2.json` (ids `cs-`). Series: XLP; PBJ plus baskets for household/personal products and staples retail.*

## Preamble, 1999-2005: from pariah to refuge

Staples are what investors own when they are afraid, and 1999 showed the other side: money left slow-growing food, household and tobacco companies to chase internet stocks, and staples fell in a year the Nasdaq rose 85.6%. Tobacco was the extreme, de-rated to record lows by the 1998 Master Settlement and the July 14 2000 Engle verdict ($145 billion punitive, $74 billion attributed to Philip Morris USA). The reversal began in March 2000 (Nasdaq -2.6%, S&P 500 +2.4%) and staples held value through the 2000-2002 bear market. The period ended at peak confidence in branded pricing power: P&G's roughly $54 billion purchase of Gillette (January 2005, cleared September 30 2005) was the largest consumer-products deal to date.

## 2006-2009: the crisis trade

The 2008 crash was staples' finest hour in relative terms. XLP was the best-performing sector fund in both halves of 2008, down only mid-single digits by October while the S&P 500 was down 35-40%, as investors crowded into non-cyclical earnings and dividends; Walmart grew revenue 7% to $401 billion as shoppers traded down and was one of the few mega-caps to rise. The flip side arrived immediately: in the 2009 risk rally staples lagged (down 9.7% versus the S&P's 8.9% by late March 2009). The pattern, outperform in the drawdown and lag in the recovery, is the sector's defining factor loading and has repeated in 2011, 2018, 2020, 2022 and 2026.

## 2010-2016: the bond-proxy era

With rates at zero, staples were bought as bond substitutes. Dividend yield, low volatility and emerging-market consumer growth drove a five-year re-rating in which P&G, Coca-Cola and the food companies traded at premium multiples despite low single-digit growth, peaking in mid-2016 when the 10-year yield touched its lows. Financial engineering filled the growth gap: Berkshire and 3G's Heinz deal (2013) and the Kraft Heinz merger (2015) promised that zero-based budgeting could manufacture margin indefinitely, and the sub-industry's valuation came to depend on that promise.

## 2017-2019: the de-rating

The promise broke on three fronts. Rates: XLP fell 2.1% in 2017 while SPY gained 15.4%, then dropped 13% by mid-May 2018 as the 10-year yield rose from 2.4% to 3.1%, on track for its worst year since 2008's 17% decline. Amazon: the June 16 2017 Whole Foods deal took 9.2% off Kroger, 7.2% off Costco, 5.2% off Target and 4.7% off Walmart in a day, about $29 billion, and forced grocers into a multi-year margin-destroying investment in delivery and price. Regulation and accounting: the FDA's July 28 2017 proposal to cut nicotine to non-addictive levels cut Altria 17% in a day, and on February 22 2019 Kraft Heinz disclosed a $15.4 billion brand writedown, a dividend cut and an SEC subpoena and fell 28% to a record low, discrediting the 3G model. By 2019 packaged food was being valued as a collection of melting ice cubes, while Walmart and Costco were pulling away as the sub-industry's only growth stories.

## 2020-2022: hoarding, inflation and the retail margin shock

COVID briefly made staples momentum stocks. Panic buying lifted the packaged-food index 4.7% on March 2 2020, Clorox rose 7.9% in a day (most since 2011) and about 20% in a month, and Kroger was up 15% year-to-date on March 18 while the S&P 500 was down 26%. Leadership passed to reopening names in November 2020, and the 2021-2022 inflation then tested pricing power: the food and household companies pushed through double-digit price increases and staples outperformed sharply in the 2022 bear market, but the retailers could not keep up. Walmart fell 11% on May 17 2022 and Target 25% the next day, its worst day since 1987, as inflated inventory and fuel costs halved Target's operating margin to 5.3%. The consumer's rotation from goods to food and fuel favoured Walmart's grocery mix, which is why Walmart, not Target, emerged as the decade's winner.

## 2023-2024: the GLP-1 scare, China beauty and the low-income consumer

October 4 2023 was the sub-industry's most important day in a decade: Walmart's US CEO said GLP-1 users were already buying less, the staples gauge fell 2.7% on the Friday for its worst week since September 2022, and over the following year the five largest pure-play packaged-food stocks fell about 18% while the S&P 500 rose 24%. The fear faded in 2024 but the de-rating stuck. In household and personal products the casualty was Estee Lauder, which fell 18.9% on November 1 2023 on the failed China travel-retail recovery and lost more than $100 billion of value from its January 2022 peak. In staples retail the divide widened: Dollar General fell 32% on August 29 2024 (its worst day since IPO) as its low-income customer ran out of money and Walmart took share, and Walgreens, down 55% in the first half of 2024 and ejected from the Dow for Amazon, ended as a $10 billion Sycamore take-private in 2025.

## 2025: MAHA, tariffs, restructurings and the Tylenol shock

Policy moved into the aisle. HHS announced on April 22 2025 a phase-out of six synthetic dyes by end-2026; the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 4) cut SNAP by $186 billion over ten years and widened work requirements, a slow headwind for dollar stores, discount grocers and the packaged-food volumes they carry; and the November 6 GLP-1 pricing deal ($245 a month for Medicare and Medicaid, Medicare obesity coverage, sub-$350 TrumpRx prices) revived the 2023 volume fears. The food and beverage companies responded with restructuring: Keurig Dr Pepper fell 11% on August 25 on its $18 billion JDE Peet's purchase and planned split, and Kraft Heinz announced on September 2 that it would undo its 2015 merger with the shares down 60% since. The year's strangest event was in household products: Trump's September 22 2025 claim linking Tylenol to autism sent Kenvue sharply lower, and on November 3 Kimberly-Clark agreed to buy it for $48.7 billion at a 46% premium, with Kenvue up 18% and Kimberly-Clark down 12.5% on the day. The deal closed on November 2 2026.

## 2026: the defensive rotation

Staples are having their best year since XLP launched in 1998. The fund was up about 13% by late February, the strongest start in its history, and reached a record high on November 4 2026, up roughly 17% year-to-date while consumer discretionary was flat and the S&P 500 lagged, as money rotated into Walmart, Costco, Coca-Cola and P&G on consumer-stress, tariff and AI-capex worries. The caution is that by February the sector already traded at its most expensive relative valuation in about 25 years on flat 2025 earnings, so the move is a positioning and rates story, not an earnings story, and the 2009, 2016 and 2020 precedents say it reverses when risk appetite returns.

## What the sub-industries trade on today

**Food, beverage and tobacco (PBJ).** The group trades on GLP-1 volume risk (re-opened by the November 2025 price cuts and TrumpRx), MAHA regulation (dye phase-out by end-2026, SNAP restrictions, ultra-processed-food labelling), private-label share gains and retailer pressure, and self-help restructurings (Kraft Heinz split, KDP's coffee separation). Tobacco trades on nicotine-pouch growth versus menthol and nicotine-cap risk, and the whole group on its dividend yield relative to Treasuries, which is why it is leading in 2026's defensive rotation.

**Household and personal products.** Pricing-versus-volume elasticity after three years of price increases is the fundamental question; input costs (pulp, resin) and the dollar are the margin swing factors. Kimberly-Clark's integration of Kenvue and the Tylenol litigation overhang are the group's idiosyncratic stories; Estee Lauder and the beauty names remain hostage to China and travel retail; and the sub-industry carries the largest "bond proxy" sensitivity to the 10-year yield.

**Staples retail and distribution.** The group has bifurcated. Walmart and Costco trade on share gains from trade-down, membership-fee economics, grocery inflation and their ability to absorb tariffs on general merchandise, and they are the main reason XLP is at records. Dollar stores, drugstores and Kroger-type grocers trade on SNAP cuts, the stretched low-income consumer and reimbursement pressure; Target sits in between as the discretionary-heavy laggard.
